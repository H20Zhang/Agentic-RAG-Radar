# Agentic RAG Weekly — 2026-W32

> **Coverage:** 2026-08-03 to 2026-08-09 · **Status:** finalized, revised 2026-08-12 · **Adjacent context:** ACE-GraphRAG (Aug 2), LLM-Wiki (May), and Know Before You Fetch (June) are used only where they change the interpretation of this week's work.

## Week thesis

**W32 is better understood as a convergence and stress-test of Agentic RAG's control stack—not the moment the field suddenly moved beyond top-k.**

The week makes four control variables unusually explicit: **operation/interface**, **evidence state**, **context policy**, and **retrieval budget**. READ and DocNavRAG sharpen the interface/state story; SAGE makes per-query resource allocation a learned policy; the IR-history perspective simultaneously raises the novelty bar by showing that closed-loop retrieval, query refinement, and answer verification have classical QA antecedents.

The resulting research question is harder than “does an agent help?”:

> **What does modern model-based control add after we match the information interface, resource budget, and historical prior art?**

## Three durable shifts

### 1. Retrieval control is becoming factorized

**READ** changes the primitive from dense top-k to lexical search, structural navigation, and bounded read. **DocNavRAG** adds document-native navigation plus explicit collected/missing evidence state. **ACE-GraphRAG** makes context assembly over a rich graph a policy rather than fixed post-processing.

The common stack is increasingly visible:

`substrate → operation set → state → policy → stopping`

This is a useful shift because those layers can finally be ablated independently. But the chronology matters: **A-RAG and LLM-Wiki already made the agent-facing retrieval-interface argument earlier in 2026**. LLM-Wiki is especially important because keeping its compiled Wiki while disabling progressive traversal reportedly costs 11.7–13.8 F1, giving direct evidence that runtime composition adds value beyond the richer substrate.

### 2. “Budget” is becoming an action, not only an evaluation metric

**SAGE** chooses a per-query passage budget under an explicit latency SLO. This makes `how much retrieval?` a first-class policy decision rather than a global knob.

The adjacent June paper **Know Before You Fetch** makes the evaluation standard stricter: retrieval-call rate, full-context rate, passage budget, and measured latency are different resources. Its negative result—that `k=1` can reduce passage volume without reducing retrieval-call rate, and that a confidence probe can make gating slower in some model regimes—means “adaptive retrieval saves cost” is too vague a claim.

SAGE's production framing is useful, but its strongest experiments compare with **static-k** baselines. Adaptive-k and Stop-RAG are discussed but not directly benchmarked. The next bar is adaptive-vs-adaptive under the same substrate and SLO.

### 3. The novelty baseline just got older

**Forgotten History or Test-of-Time?** argues that modern Agentic RAG inherits important problem structures from classical IR/QA. Its most concrete case is QUALIFIER: structured constraints, retrieve/extract, answer verification, successive constraint relaxation, and repeated search before answer/NIL.

This does **not** mean modern work is merely rediscovery. It changes the `compared with what` question. The novelty may live in LLM-capable state representations, richer action interfaces, learned policies, scale, or transfer—not in the existence of an iterative retrieve→check→reformulate loop itself.

The paper reports QUALIFIER's TREC 2002 `pris2002` run at 290/500 correct, second to LCC's 415/500. Its claim that iterative refinement helps explain the ranking is retrospective, however, not a matched causal ablation. Historical precedent is strong; causal equivalence is not established.

## Papers that matter most

### 1. Forgotten History or Test-of-Time? — ★★★★☆

**Delta:** raises the novelty baseline from “recent RAG” to classical IR/QA.

**So what:** future papers on query refinement, verification, or adaptive retries should specify what is new beyond the control-loop shape.

**Evidence caveat:** perspective/systematization, not exhaustive meta-analysis; historical performance is not a modern ablation.

### 2. Beyond Top-K / READ — ★★★★☆

**Delta:** replaces opaque dense top-k with replayable lexical/structural/read operations.

**So what:** the same agent loop cannot rescue a bad primitive.

**Evidence caveat:** BM25 is reported statistically indistinguishable from READ, so the strongest conclusion is about the retrieval primitive/interface—not a universal benefit from agentic composition.

### 3. DocNavRAG — ★★★★☆

**Delta:** co-designs document navigation with explicit evidence-progress state.

**So what:** state becomes a systems interface, not just prompt history.

**Evidence caveat:** structure, evidence state, and variable retrieval budget still need cleaner separation.

### 4. SAGE — ★★★☆☆

**Delta:** learns per-query retrieval budget under an explicit P95 SLO with no extra LLM call.

**So what:** resource allocation joins tool choice and stopping as a controller action.

**Evidence caveat:** on the main 334-query NQ table, SAGE reaches 95% SLO compliance / 3.6 s P95 / 22% EM / average k=9.8 versus static k=20 at 30% / 5.6 s / 24% / k=20, but contemporary adaptive controllers are not directly compared.

### Adjacent: ACE-GraphRAG — ★★★★☆

**Delta:** representation quality and query-time context policy are explicitly separated.

**Why adjacent:** Aug 2 publication, but it completes the week's interface→state→policy picture.

## Core tension: better agent, better environment, or better resource allocation?

The week's papers make a single end-to-end score increasingly insufficient. A gain may come from a different substrate, richer operations, more explicit state, a better policy, more retrieval budget—or simply a better classical primitive such as BM25.

The minimum credible lens is now:

`substrate × operation set × state × policy × budget × base model × historical baseline`

A policy claim should control the first five as much as possible. A novelty claim should also explain what the modern system adds beyond earlier closed-loop IR/QA patterns.

## Evidence audit

Three evidence weaknesses remain load-bearing:

1. **Interface confounding.** READ's BM25 result and LLM-Wiki's structure/traversal ablation point in opposite but complementary directions: sometimes the primitive dominates; sometimes active traversal adds large value. We need both ablations in the same studies.
2. **Resource accounting.** Calls, passages/tokens, latency, and controller/probe overhead must be separated. Know Before You Fetch shows why one “cost” scalar can hide reversals.
3. **Adaptive-baseline weakness.** SAGE shows a strong static-k frontier improvement but does not directly establish superiority over recent adaptive-k/stopping controllers.

## Minimal reading order

**LLM-Wiki → READ → DocNavRAG** teaches environment/interface and state.  
**Know Before You Fetch → SAGE** teaches budget control and why cost axes matter.  
**Forgotten History or Test-of-Time?** should be read last to recalibrate novelty claims across all of them.

## Questions to carry forward

1. **Can one benchmark factor interface, state, policy, and budget independently?** Otherwise “agentic” remains a bundle of simultaneous improvements.
2. **Can adaptive controllers beat strong adaptive—not static—baselines under matched hardware and resource axes?** This is now the decisive systems comparison.
3. **Which parts of modern Agentic RAG are genuinely new capabilities versus scalable implementations of old IR/QA control principles?** The answer should be empirical, not rhetorical.
