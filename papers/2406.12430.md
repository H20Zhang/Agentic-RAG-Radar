## Problem

Decision-making questions can require several dependent data analyses. A one-shot retriever cannot express those dependencies, while a loosely iterative retrieval loop may repeatedly search without an explicit model of what information remains necessary for the decision.

## Core idea

Generate a high-level plan for the decision task first, then derive data queries and analysis steps from that plan. Retrieval is therefore subordinate to an explicit information-acquisition structure.

## Agent loop

`Plan → formulate data query → retrieve/analyze → update reasoning → repeat → decide`

## Retrieval design

Retrieval is used to acquire database evidence required by plan steps rather than simply fetching semantically similar text. The key controller is the explicit plan.

## Compared to what

Single-step RAG and iterative RAG where retrieval proceeds without a stable high-level plan describing what information needs to be acquired.

## Evidence

The paper introduces a Decision QA benchmark (DQA) and reports gains over an iterative RAG baseline in two scenarios. The radar has not yet re-checked the full tables, planner ablations, or generalization beyond the specialized decision-QA setting.

## Why it matters

PlanRAG is useful less as the current state of the art and more as an **early control-stack design point**: it makes planning an explicit upstream component of retrieval. Later Agentic RAG work can be understood as relaxing or learning different pieces of this control loop.

It also matters for database-facing agents because the retrieval action is closer to structured data acquisition than ordinary passage similarity search.

## Limitations / questions

- Is the plan genuinely necessary, or can a strong iterative reasoner infer the same information needs online?
- How brittle is the plan when early assumptions or retrieved facts are wrong?
- Does explicit planning help outside Decision QA/database workloads?
- How much extra latency/token cost is introduced by planning before retrieval?

**Curator take:** **3/5**. A useful historical/design anchor for explicit information-acquisition planning; narrower and less evidentially current than the newer interface/state/policy papers.
