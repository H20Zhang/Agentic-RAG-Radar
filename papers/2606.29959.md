## Problem

Fixed retrieval wastes context on queries the reader already knows and can hurt through distractors. But binary retrieval gating is also too coarse: it treats `k=1` and `k=5` as the same retrieval decision even though they consume very different context budgets.

A second problem is calibration. A raw uncertainty score may rank queries well but still be difficult to threshold across datasets, readers, or multiple retrieval actions.

## Core idea

Run a short closed-book probe, map its uncertainty signal to a calibrated probability of correctness, and allocate one of three context budgets:

`k=0 → k=1 → k=5`

A selective variant can abstain when the confidence of the answer actually chosen remains too low.

## Agent loop

This is a one-step controller rather than a long-horizon agent:

`closed-book probe → calibrated probability → choose no/compact/full retrieval → answer or abstain`

## Retrieval design

The reader and retriever remain frozen. The controller is a calibration-and-threshold layer. The paper deliberately reports several resource axes separately:

- retrieval-call rate;
- full-context rate;
- normalized passage budget;
- measured end-to-end latency.

That distinction is the paper's most reusable systems contribution.

## Compared to what

The experiments include always-retrieve, binary gating, TARG-style entropy/margin signals, and matched-reader decision-table versions of Self-RAG/Adaptive-RAG-inspired gates. The paper is explicit that these are decision-signal comparisons rather than full-pipeline reproductions of every prior system.

## Evidence

The paper reports large reductions in calibration error on TriviaQA, Natural Questions, and MS MARCO. Graded allocation improves passage-budget and full-context frontiers, but **does not improve retrieval-call AUC much over binary gating** because `k=1` still counts as a retrieval call.

The measured cost analysis is unusually useful: at matched-accuracy frontier points, gating is reported about **27% slower on Qwen3-8B** but about **8% faster on Qwen3-32B**. The sign flips because the closed-book probe is real generation work.

## Why it matters

This is a strong baseline for any later paper claiming adaptive retrieval efficiency. A method should say which resource it saves: calls, passages/tokens, tail latency, or money. Those are not interchangeable.

It also makes SAGE easier to judge: SAGE adds an explicit production-SLO objective and a learned retrieval-side policy, but adaptive budget allocation itself already has a disciplined June 2026 baseline.

## Limitations / questions

- Does the calibrated controller remain stable under corpus/model/query-distribution drift?
- How does the discrete `0/1/5` action space extend to multi-round retrieval where evidence changes the next decision?
- When the confidence probe is expensive, can retrieval-side features or cached signals preserve the same calibration quality without the extra generation pass?

**Curator take:** **4/5**. Important because the cost accounting is sharper than the headline adaptive-RAG story, and because it establishes a difficult baseline for future budget-allocation claims.
